Introduction

A coffee setup is a collection of small rituals. Aer explores how a milk frother could feel at home beside Fellow’s Aiden and Stagg: considered in its form, tactile in its controls, and clear in its feedback.

A personal project by Jared Teiger · YES AND DESIGN STUDIO
Independent exploration inspired by Fellow’s design language.

My contribution

Research, sketching, CAD, 3D printing, interface design, Arduino programming, and visual storytelling.

Finding a Place on the Counter

Why use a different design language for the next step in the ritual?

The starting question was personal: why pair a Nespresso milk frother with an Aiden and Stagg kettle? I used that mismatch as an invitation to explore a more coherent coffee setup.

A market map helped me compare simplicity and complexity across casual and enthusiast products. Studying Fellow objects alongside Heath Ceramics gave me a reference for restrained forms, material character, and everyday use.

Sketching the Possibilities

A familiar family of forms.
A new part of the routine.

The Base & the Controls

A simple gesture, with room for choice.

I explored different ways to balance a quiet appearance with useful control. An analog approach felt direct and tactile, but limited how much the user could customize.

The direction became a rotary encoder paired with a circular display: one physical control, with the screen changing to match each step.

From CAD to Something I Could Hold

The vessel moved through smooth cylinders, fluted surfaces, and handled variations. Printing at full scale made those differences tangible and brought the handle and overall proportions into the same conversation.

The strongest shift was toward restraint. A simple form could stand on its own; fluting could become an accent at the base instead of defining the entire object.

Two Ways to Hold the Idea

Precision in the details. Simplicity in the silhouette.

The handled direction brought a defined pouring gesture and a fluted lower section. The handleless direction kept the body quiet, with the seam between cup and lower housing doing more of the visual work.

Both shared the same compact base and dial. Keeping that foundation consistent let the vessel carry the difference in character.

Designing the Interaction

Choose. Adjust. Follow along.

Conversations with baristas at Ritual Coffee informed the milk-selection concept. Icons, color, and a circular progress indicator give each stage its own clear visual cue.

  1. 01

    Choose the milk

    Rotate through regular, oat/soy, almond, half-and-half, and a mix mode. Press to continue.

  2. 02

    Choose the temperature

    The concept proposes adjustable temperature settings. The coded prototype demonstrates the selection step using two color-coded choices.

  3. 03

    See the progress

    A countdown and a changing ring show the interaction moving toward completion.

Making the Interface Work

From drawn screens to a responsive object.

I programmed the interface in Arduino on an ESP32, using a rotary encoder to navigate the round color display. Converting PNG artwork into 16-bit C arrays let the custom graphics run directly on the hardware.

The working prototype moves through milk selection, a temperature-choice screen, and countdown feedback. Building it made the relationship between the physical dial and the screen something I could experience.

This prototype demonstrates the interface. Heating, motor control, and temperature sensing remain part of the next engineering phase.

Looking Inside the Object

Taking apart a milk frother helped me understand the components behind the experience. I studied how the motor, power system, and heating assembly occupy space, then used CAD to explore the internal arrangement.

The printed shell was a tool for studying fit and spacing. A functioning heated cup would require different materials and further mechanical and thermal development.

Questioning the Whisk

Could the mixing geometry be different?

The familiar spring coil became another starting question. I explored an alternative whisk geometry through modeling and printed studies.

This remains a design hypothesis. Foam quality, fluid behavior, and manufacturability still need testing before the geometry can be evaluated against an existing whisk.

Meet Aer

Two expressions of the same everyday ritual.

Confidence in Precision
A defined handle, pouring lip, and fluted lower section give the first direction a more articulated character.

Elegance in Simplicity
The second direction reduces the vessel to a smooth, continuous silhouette above the shared base.

Extending the Story to the Box

The packaging exploration carries the product silhouette onto a simple kraft-colored surface. Flowing linework suggests heating and mixing, while the dark product image keeps the object at the center of the story.

It is an exploration of how Aer could be introduced as part of a product family.

From Prototype Toward Product

The next questions are engineering questions.

Mechanical & thermal integration

Develop the heated cup and rotary drive as an integrated system within the compact form.

Whisk validation

Test the proposed geometry, compare its frothing performance, and assess how it could be made.

Manufacturing development

Resolve materials, tooling, and assembly for the cup and electronics housing.

Aer brought form development, electronics, code, and storytelling into one project. The result is a set of physical and interface prototypes, with a clear path for the work still needed to make the full product function.
