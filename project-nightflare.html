Introduction

Nightflare explores how active lighting can become part of high-visibility clothing for first responders. The vest combines neon and black fabric, retroreflective details, and a removable LED unit—developed through material experiments, garment construction, and feedback from Atlanta firefighters.

Starting at the Station

Understand what responders already use.

I began by observing the equipment firefighters use day to day, looking specifically at products with lighting built into their function.

Conversations with firefighters raised an opportunity: explore lighting within the safety vest itself for nighttime roadway incidents. I then looked at existing high-visibility vests and how other products incorporated light.

The Vision

Bring light into the vest while keeping the person wearing it at the center of the design.

Exploring the Visual Language

I introduced black alongside the familiar neon and grey palette. The darker areas created negative space, breaking up the vest and giving the reflective pattern a clearer relationship to the body.

Concept studies explored the balance between the shoulders, torso, and waist, alongside different ways of fastening the vest.

Material & Lighting Trials

Test the promising ideas early, then let the results change the direction.

I first tried electroluminescent tape for its flexibility and low profile. Its brightness did not meet what I was looking for in these trials, so I moved to LEDs.

Magnetic buckles were another early experiment. They felt cumbersome and slow to put on during testing, which helped narrow the fastening direction.

Learning Through Making

My first sewn vest started with paper, fabric, and trial and error.

I had no prior sewing experience when I began this project. Making paper patterns helped me understand how individual flat panels could come together around the body.

Assembling the first vest gave me something tangible to refine: panel proportions, reflective placement, and a surface onto which I could attach the lighting.

Making the Light Wearable

A soft housing for the electronics.

A class brainstorm led to what we called “LED ravioli”: sandwiching the LEDs between black backing cloth and a white diffuser. This gave me a way to incorporate the strips into a soft garment.

I coded four lighting options—blue, red, white, and a flashing red-and-blue mode—and explored laser-cut patterns in the reflective material. The first vest became a platform for trying both the lighting and the visual language.

Returning for Feedback

The biggest shift came from how firefighters wanted to use and maintain the vest.

After completing the first vest, I took it to Station 11. The feedback moved the project beyond appearance and into everyday handling.

Too many pieces to remove
Detaching individual LED elements felt like a hassle. I consolidated them into one compact unit.
Focus the lighting on the back
Firefighters saw little need for side LEDs and recommended rear lighting. Opinions on a front light were mixed.
Consider the rest of the equipment
They raised charging habits, radio attachment, and adjustability—questions that informed the next build and its future improvements.

The Next Iteration

Simplify the back. Consolidate the lights. Make the unit removable.

I felt the earlier back design was too crowded. In the next iteration, black panels emphasized the shoulders while the reflective strips followed the shape of the lower torso.

The lighting moved into a compact pack attached to the back with hook-and-loop fasteners. The shoulder arrangement remained, with two additional strips extending into the lower part of the unit.

The Final Prototype

A sewn garment and a working lighting unit, developed as one wearable system.

From the studio back to the station.

The final vest uses neon and black cotton canvas with retroreflective details. Its lighting unit combines NeoPixel LED strips, a control button, and a rechargeable portable battery.

Photographing the prototype with firefighters brought the garment back into the context that started the project: people moving around equipment, vehicles, and changing light.

With thanks to Atlanta Fire Department Stations 11 and 15 for their time, feedback, and participation.

What I Would Develop Next

Design for the repair, too.

When a wire disconnected inside the integrated unit, getting to it required opening the assembly. That experience made access to the electronics an important next step.

My final review identified further work on battery access, waterproofing, front-to-back weight balance, radio-clip placement, and the visibility of the power button. I also wanted to study light placement when the wearer bends or kneels.

Those next steps focus on how the vest is worn, powered, and maintained.
