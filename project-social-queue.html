Introduction

What if waiting in line gave people something to share? Social Queue turns a familiar queueing post into a playful robotic object. Its bending tentacle invites people to reach out, experiment, and interact with one another.

A team project by Jared Teiger, Yixiao Zhang, and Sadie Martin

My contribution

I contributed most heavily to prototyping, 3D modeling, and coding the robot for the experiment. I also supported the initial research and helped conduct the testing.

The Starting Question

Can an ordinary waiting line become a more social place?

Our team set out to explore whether robotic interaction could make waiting more enjoyable and change how long a wait felt. The queueing post offered a familiar starting point: already visible, within reach, and part of the space.

We developed working prototypes and built a full waiting-line setup to study what people would do with them. The project combined physical making with observation, interviews, and analysis.

Giving the Post a Personality

We explored spinning, bending, and a whack-a-mole interaction. Each concept used movement to invite a different response from the person waiting beside it.

The first prototypes focused on mechanical feasibility. The whack-a-mole direction proved difficult to make robust, so we brought the spinning and bending concepts forward for testing.

Letting People Interpret the Object

The bending pole opened up more ways to play.

We tested both concepts with 14 people on campus. Participants interacted freely, described the prototypes, and rated their level of interaction.

The bending pole received an average rating of 3.8 out of 5, compared with 2.8 for the spinning pole. People described it as curious, silly, and playful, and some began to imagine games involving others in line.

That response shifted our direction. We moved away from prescribing one emotion and kept the form abstract, leaving room for people to interpret its behavior.

Building the Environment

To test the experience, we needed to build the line around it.

We made custom stanchions to control the height, spacing, and geometry of the setup. PVC pipes and CNC-cut MDF bases formed the structure; 3D-printed tops held the queueing belts.

The robotic version had to fit into the same visual language. Its housing brought the electronics inside the post, making the tentacle the focus of the interaction.

Refining the Mechanism

From a tabletop experiment to a compact, repeatable movement.

My work centered on prototyping, 3D modeling, and coding the robot. As a team, we tested different spine materials, motor arrangements, and cable layouts, replacing cardboard vertebrae with printed parts as the prototype became more compact.

The final version used two servos to pull cables through the vertebrae: one for forward and backward motion, the other for side-to-side movement. A flexible pipe-cleaner spine allowed the tentacle to bend.

Making Everything Fit

A two-inch envelope for the mechanism.

The post set a tight constraint for the motors, controller, and battery. Modeling the internal assembly helped us organize the components and align the cable-driven mechanism.

We also explored camera-based hand tracking. For the final study, we prioritized two working prototypes and used concealed joystick control—a “Wizard of Oz” setup in which a team member operated each robot.

Three Ways to Engage

The same form could invite, follow, or retreat.

Putting the Line to the Test

Observe the interaction in context, then ask people about the experience.

Two conditions. The same waiting task.

We recruited 29 participants for the robot condition and 10 for a control condition without the robots. In the robot condition, one tentacle used Escape behavior and the other used Friendly behavior; either could switch to Attract between interactions.

Participants joined a staged waiting line and completed an interview afterward. We reviewed video observations and interview responses to understand play, social interaction, enjoyment, and perceived waiting time.

What People Made of It

The most interesting interactions were the ones we hadn’t scripted.

What We Learned

01

Watching was an invitation.

People learned from seeing someone else interact. Observation could become curiosity, then a first attempt at play.

02

Simple behavior left room for invention.

Participants tried pointing, grabbing, using both hands, and moving around the tentacle. Some created a rock-paper-scissors game or danced with it.

03

Play could become social.

People compared ideas, taught others how to play, and coordinated their movements. The object became a shared point of interest.

Reading the Results

Promising interactions.
An open research question.

Our observations suggested opportunities for enjoyment and social connection. They did not establish that Social Queue shortened perceived waiting time: participants in both conditions often underestimated their wait.

The study used a small campus sample and a staged queue. Testing in naturally occurring lines, with broader participants and a stronger way to measure time perception, would help us understand where the idea works best.
